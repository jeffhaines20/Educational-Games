# Source notes — what the two lectures teach

A digest of the course's two PowerPoint decks, with slide numbers, for writing and
checking cases. **L1** = `Cardiovascular system Pathology (1).pptx` (66 slides);
**L2** = `Patho cardio conditions 2 (1).pptx` (73 slides). "s12" = slide 12. Figures that
are pictures in the decks (tables, charts) are transcribed here.

The decks are written for physical therapist assistant (PTA) students: they repeatedly say
what "the PTA" should do and give "implications for PT" for every drug class.

## 0. Where the game follows current practice instead of the slide

Cases never use these slide statements as an answer key; where a case touches them, the
explanation gives the current view.

| Slide | Says | Current practice the game uses |
|---|---|---|
| L1 s31 | Digitalis "slow[s] down the atrial contractions and thus restore[s] them to functional contraction" | Digoxin slows conduction through the AV node, so the **ventricular rate** is controlled; it does not restore sinus rhythm. |
| L1 s37 | Complete heart block makes the heart "skip a beat" | In third-degree block no atrial impulse reaches the ventricles; a slow escape rhythm takes over. Dropped ("skipped") beats describe second-degree block. |
| L1 s41 | PVCs "are potentially life threatening and require immediate attention from the physician" | Isolated PVCs are common and often benign (caffeine, anxiety, smoking); the warning signs are the ones on L1 s20: more than 5/min during exercise, pairs, multifocal — or symptoms. |
| L2 s32 | Cardiac enzymes CPK, AST, LDH | Troponin is today's main blood marker of heart-muscle injury; the slide's enzymes may be mentioned alongside it. |
| L2 s32 | "estrogen (in women)" in MI management | Not recommended; never presented as treatment. |
| L2 s33 | Exercise testing "within 3 days of the MI" | Not used as a question. |
| L2 s60 | DVT: "the patient is required to have bed rest" | Once anticoagulation is under way, early walking is encouraged. Cases ask the PTA to stop exercise and get the patient medically evaluated, not to order bed rest. |
| L2 s13 | "Atherosclerosis literally means hardening of the arteries" | Atherosclerosis = fatty plaque (atheroma) in artery walls; arteriosclerosis = hardening/thickening of artery walls. Both narrow the lumen. |
| L2 s22 | Stage 2 "SBP 140 DBP 90"; crisis "SBP 180 DBP 120" | As the AHA chart on L2 s23 says: stage 2 is ≥ 140 **or** ≥ 90; crisis is > 180 **and/or** > 120. |
| L2 s27 | MI statistics (1.5 million a year, leading cause of death) | Not used. |
| L2 s68 | "pulmonary hypotension" (typo) | Pulmonary **hypertension**. |
| L2 s50 | ABI above 1.30 = non-compressible; 1.00–1.29 normal | Current AHA/ACC guidelines: above **1.40** = non-compressible; 1.00–1.40 normal. Cases and drills avoid ABIs between 1.30 and 1.40, and the Casebook mentions both cut-offs. |
| L2 s45 | Plaque damages the walls of "both arteries and veins" | Atherosclerosis is a disease of arteries. Not used as a question. |
| L1 s38 | Claudication "most often" from popliteal artery disease | The femoral and popliteal arteries are the usual sites; cases say "femoral and popliteal". |
| L2 s49 | Anticoagulants and thrombolytics for PVD | Standard long-term treatment is an antiplatelet plus a statin; clot-dissolving drugs are for a sudden arterial blockage. The Casebook says so. |
| L2 s51 | "At least 30 %" 5-year mortality in symptomatic PVD | Current estimates vary (roughly 15–30 %); cases say "up to about 30 %". |
| L1 s20 | "Bindle branch" | Bundle branch block. |

## 1. Anatomy and physiology (L1 s2–19)

* Cardinal symptoms of cardiac disease (s2): chest, neck or arm pain/discomfort;
  palpitations; dyspnea; syncope; fatigue; cough; cyanosis. Vascular: edema and leg pain
  (claudication). Systems table (s4): general (weakness, fatigue, weight change, poor
  exercise tolerance), skin (pressure ulcers, loss of body hair, cyanosis of lips and nail
  beds), CNS (headache, impaired vision, light-headedness/syncope), respiratory (labored
  breathing, productive cough), cardiovascular (chest/shoulder/neck/jaw/arm pain, palpitations,
  peripheral edema, claudication), genitourinary (frequency, nocturia, concentrated urine,
  decreased output), musculoskeletal (fatigue, myalgia, peripheral edema, claudication), GI
  (nausea and vomiting, ascites).
* Angina (s2): discomfort behind the breastbone when the heart muscle lacks oxygen; may
  spread to arm, shoulder, neck, jaw, throat, back; pressure/squeezing/tightness; can be
  mistaken for indigestion; with SOB, weakness, light-headedness, sweating.
* Palpitations (s3, s40): irregular/fast/"extra" beats. Benign causes: mitral valve
  prolapse, caffeine, anxiety, exercise, athlete's heart (larger LV mass from training).
  Serious causes: CAD, cardiomyopathy, complete heart block, ventricular aneurysm, AV valve
  disease, mitral or aortic stenosis.
* Dyspnea (s3, s33) can be cardiac or pulmonary, or from trauma, fever, medication, obesity.
  Severe or at-rest dyspnea → physician referral; watch for it during treatment and allow rest.
* Chambers and valves (s6–8): RA receives deoxygenated blood from SVC (upper body) and IVC
  (lower body) → tricuspid valve (3 cusps) → RV → pulmonary (semilunar) valve → pulmonary
  artery (the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood) → lungs → pulmonary veins (carry
  oxygenated blood) → LA → mitral (bicuspid) valve → LV (thick muscular wall) → aortic
  valve → aorta. Valves open toward the next chamber and close behind the contracting one.
  Interatrial and interventricular septa divide the sides.
* Coronary arteries (s9; L2 s16–17): right and left coronary arteries arise from the
  ascending aorta just above the aortic valve. Left main → left anterior descending (LAD) and
  left circumflex; right coronary → marginal branch and posterior descending. Coronary veins
  → coronary sinus → right atrium.
* Neural control (s10): cardiac control centre in the medulla; baroreceptors in the aorta and
  carotids sense pressure. Sympathetic (epinephrine, norepinephrine) → faster and stronger;
  parasympathetic (acetylcholine via the vagus nerve) → slower, mainly via the SA node.
* Conduction (s11): SA node in the right atrium is the pacemaker; normal sinus rhythm about
  70/min. Impulse spreads over the atria → AV node (brief pause) → bundle of His → Purkinje
  fibres → both ventricles contract.
* Cardiac cycle (s12, s17): systole = contraction, diastole = relaxation and filling. AV
  valves close as the ventricles contract; semilunar valves open; then close as diastole begins.
* Preload (s13): stretch at end of diastole (end-diastolic volume, venous filling).
  Frank–Starling: more filling → more stretch → stronger contraction → larger stroke volume.
* Afterload (s14): what opposes ejection — mainly peripheral (aortic) pressure, aortic
  compliance, blood mass and viscosity; the force the LV must generate to open the aortic valve.
* Stroke volume (s14): blood ejected per LV contraction; normal 60–80 mL.
* Cardiac output (s15): CO = SV × HR (70 mL × 70/min ≈ 4.9 L/min); rest 4.5–5 L/min (men),
  slightly less in women; up to 25 L/min in exercise. Venous return equals CO over time.
* Ejection fraction (s15–16): EF = SV ÷ LV end-diastolic volume; normal > 55 % (about 58 %);
  lower EF = more impaired LV. Reduced EF (s43) lowers aerobic capacity; seen in CHF,
  hypertension, CAD, MI.
* ECG (s18–19): P wave = atrial depolarization (contraction); PR interval = impulse from
  atria through the conduction system to the Purkinje fibres; QRS = ventricular
  depolarization (contraction); ST segment = start of ventricular repolarization; T wave =
  ventricular repolarization; QT = electrical systole.
* Selected ECG abnormalities (s20): ST elevation — new infarct/injury or pericarditis; ST
  depression — may indicate ischemia if downsloping 2–3 mm; inverted T — ischemia (L2 s9:
  T inversion can be a normal variant in limb leads but is always abnormal in V2–V6); wide QRS —
  bundle branch block; Q wave — infarction; P waves different or absent — atrial or
  ventricular arrhythmias. PVCs during exercise are a concern at more than 5/min, in pairs,
  or when multifocal.

## 2. Heart sounds and auscultation (L1 s21–26)

* S1 "lubb": closing of the tricuspid and mitral (AV) valves; loudest at the apex; normal.
* S2 "dupp": closing of the semilunar (aortic and pulmonary) valves; normal.
* S3: ventricles filling in early diastole, just after S2; heard at the apex with the
  patient lying on the left side; normal in children and young adults, **abnormal in adults
  over 40** — LV failure, tachycardia, mitral regurgitation, congestive heart failure.
  (L2 s36: S3 gallop in heart failure.)
* S4 (atrial gallop): ventricles filling after atrial contraction, heard just before S1, at
  the apex; abnormal — LV hypertrophy, chronic hypertension, cardiomyopathy, MI.
* Murmurs: turbulent flow; graded I (faint) to VI (heard without a stethoscope).
* Areas (s24–26): aortic — 2nd intercostal space, right sternal border; pulmonic — 2nd ICS,
  left sternal border; Erb's point — 3rd ICS, left sternal border; tricuspid — 4th ICS, left
  sternal border; mitral — 5th ICS, left midclavicular line (the apex). Stethoscope on bare
  skin, patient breathing quietly through the nose.

## 3. General signs and symptoms (L1 s27–47)

* **Atrial fibrillation** (s27–31): disorganised atrial activity — the atria quiver instead
  of beating; ineffective atrial pumping. ECG: no recognisable P waves, an irregular wavy
  (fibrillatory) baseline, atrial rate 300–600/min, **irregular RR intervals with no pattern**.
  Loss of "atrial kick" can lower cardiac output. Flutter: atria beat too fast but
  **regularly** — a sawtooth baseline. AFib is seen with hypertension, rheumatic heart
  disease, cardiomyopathy, heart failure, after MI, COPD, diabetes, illicit drugs, digoxin
  toxicity. Signs: palpitations, irregular pulse, dyspnea, chest discomfort, dizziness,
  weakness, anxiety. **If the PTA suspects AFib, consult the supervising PT immediately**;
  CPR if the patient becomes unconscious. Treated with anti-arrhythmics; digoxin for rate
  (see §0).
* **Cyanosis** (s32): central — lips, tongue, ears, mucous membranes; more with lung disease
  and low oxygen saturation. Peripheral — fingertips, nail beds, toes, nose; more with low
  cardiac output.
* **Dyspnea** (s33): see §1.
* **Edema** (s34–35): swelling of hands, feet, ankles, abdomen, often with SOB and fatigue —
  all together suggest CHF. **Bilateral pitting edema → CHF; unilateral → local causes
  (thrombophlebitis/DVT, PVD).** Grading: **1+** barely perceptible, up to 2 mm;
  **2+** easily seen, 2–4 mm, rebounds within 15 s; **3+** 4–6 mm, rebounds in 15–30 s;
  **4+** 6–8 mm, lasts > 30 s.
* **Fatigue** (s36): with dyspnea, headache, chest pain or arrhythmia → stop the activity,
  have the patient rest, consult the PT.
* **Heart block** (s37): first degree — impulses slowed (long PR), every beat conducted;
  second degree — impulses occasionally blocked (dropped beats); third degree (complete) —
  no impulses reach the ventricles. Usually treated with a **pacemaker**.
* **Intermittent claudication** (s38): calf pain on walking/exercise/standing from arterial
  disease (often popliteal), relieved by rest; worsens as disease progresses.
* **Pain** (s39; L2 s10, s31): referred to the left shoulder, left/central chest, left neck
  and jaw; heart supplied by C5–C6 segments (C6 dermatome: thumb side of hand and forearm;
  C5: over the deltoid). Angina may be felt only in the left shoulder or along the inner arm
  (ulnar side) to the elbow.
* **Palpitations** (s40): see §1.
* **PVCs** (s41–42): the most common arrhythmia; an early beat from the ventricle; **wide
  (> 0.12 s) and bizarre QRS**, no P wave before it. **Unifocal** = the PVCs look alike;
  **multifocal** = they look different. Seen after caffeine, smoking, anxiety, alcohol; after
  MI; with CAD/CHF ischemia; electrolyte imbalance; lung disease; hypoxemia. Can progress to
  VT or VF.
* **Reduced ejection fraction** (s43): see §1.
* **Syncope** (s44): reduced oxygen to the brain → light-headedness or fainting; cardiac
  syncope when due to arrhythmia or CAD → **alert the PT, nurse or physician immediately**.
* **Ventricular fibrillation** (s45–47): chaotic ventricular activity, no effective
  contraction; fatal without immediate treatment. Causes: ischemia/acute MI/CAD, respiratory
  failure (hypoxia), massive PE, electric shock, advanced heart block. Treatment:
  **defibrillation with CPR**, oxygen and medications; all staff, including PTAs, are trained
  in CPR and the defibrillator.

## 4. Diagnostic tests (L1 s48–50; L2 throughout)

* **Holter (ambulatory ECG)**: 24–48 h or longer recording with a symptom diary — rhythm,
  medication effect, pacemaker function.
* **12-lead ECG**: primary tool for angina (inverted T = ischemia) and MI (L2 s8, s32).
* **Angiography / cardiac catheterization**: catheter from leg or arm artery to the
  coronaries, contrast dye; location and severity of narrowing (most accurate test for CAD,
  L2 s19); pressures and oxygen in the heart.
* **CT**, **MRI**: cross-sectional images; CT/MR coronary angiograms are less invasive.
* **Echocardiography**: ultrasound images in real time — chamber size and function, wall and
  septum thickness, valves, ejection fraction (Doppler echo: systolic/diastolic performance,
  filling pressures).
* **Fluoroscopy**: continuous X-ray; mostly replaced by echo.
* **Chest X-ray**: confirms fluid in the lungs (pulmonary edema); part of the CHF work-up.
* **Blood tests**: cardiac enzymes (§0); CHF work-up: urinalysis, CBC, electrolytes, TSH,
  BUN, creatinine; PVD: CBC, BUN, electrolytes.
* **Doppler ultrasound**: blood flow; DVT and PVD. **Venous ultrasonography** for CVI.
* **Ankle-brachial index** (L2 s48–50): see §9.
* **V/Q (ventilation–perfusion) scan and CT**: confirm PE.
* **Transcutaneous oximetry, rubor of dependency test, treadmill test**: PVD baseline.
* **Pulmonary function tests**: confirm lung disease in cor pulmonale.

## 5. Drug classes (L1 s51–60) — action · indications · side effects · PT implications · examples

1. **Alpha-adrenergic antagonists** — relax peripheral vessels (dilate arterioles and veins),
   lower BP · hypertension · dizziness, palpitations, **orthostatic hypotension**, drowsiness
   · rise slowly from sitting/lying; monitor during exercise · Cardura (doxazosin), Minipress
   (prazosin).
2. **ACE inhibitors** — lower BP and afterload · hypertension, CHF · hypotension, dizziness,
   **dry cough**, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia · avoid sudden posture changes; heart-failure
   patients avoid rapid increases in activity · Capoten (captopril), Vasotec (enalapril).
3. **Anticoagulants** — reduce clotting, prevent thrombus · after CABG, VTE prevention, AFib
   and prosthetic valves · **hemorrhage, bleeding**, GI upset · avoid injury (bleeding,
   bruising); educate · heparin, Coumadin (warfarin).
4. **Antihyperlipidemics (statins)** — lower LDL and triglycerides, raise HDL ·
   hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis, prevent coronary events · headache, GI distress,
   **myalgia**, rash · aerobic exercise raises HDL and helps the drug · Lipitor
   (atorvastatin), Zocor (simvastatin).
5. **Antiplatelets (antithrombotics)** — stop platelets clumping · after MI, AFib, prevent
   arterial thrombus · hemorrhage, thrombocytopenia, liver toxicity, GI distress · avoid
   injury (bleeding); educate · Bayer (aspirin), Plavix (clopidogrel).
6. **Beta blockers** — lower heart rate and contractility → less myocardial oxygen demand ·
   hypertension, angina, arrhythmias, heart failure, migraine, tremor · bradycardia,
   arrhythmia, fatigue, depression, dizziness, weakness, blurred vision · **HR and BP response
   to exercise is blunted — use rating of perceived exertion (RPE)**; watch for orthostatic
   hypotension · Tenormin (atenolol), Lopressor (metoprolol).
7. **Calcium channel blockers** — less calcium into vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation,
   less contraction and oxygen demand · hypertension, angina, arrhythmias, CHF · dizziness,
   headache, hypotension, **peripheral edema** · blunted HR/BP response; orthostatic
   hypotension; watch for CHF signs (worsening edema, dyspnea, weight gain) · Procardia
   (nifedipine), Cardizem (diltiazem).
8. **Diuretics** — more sodium and water excreted → less plasma volume, lower BP ·
   hypertension, CHF edema, pulmonary edema, glaucoma · dehydration, hypotension,
   **electrolyte imbalance**, polyuria, raised LDL, arrhythmias · dizziness and falls with
   position changes; watch for electrolyte imbalance, **muscle weakness or cramping** ·
   furosemide (Lasix), Diuril (chlorothiazide).
9. **Nitrates** — relax smooth muscle, dilate vessels → less ischemia · angina · **headache**,
   dizziness, **orthostatic hypotension**, tachycardia, nausea, vomiting · stand up slowly;
   **sublingual nitroglycerin is the preferred treatment for an acute angina attack** ·
   Nitrostat, nitroglycerin.
10. **Thrombolytics** — dissolve clots (plasminogen → plasmin) · acute MI, PE, ischemic stroke,
   arterial/venous thrombosis · **hemorrhage (including intracranial)**, allergy, arrhythmia ·
   avoid anything that could cause trauma · Kinlytic (urokinase), Activase (alteplase).

Also in the decks: **digoxin/digitalis** (cardiac glycoside) for AFib rate and CHF (L1 s31,
L2 s39). **Digitalis toxicity** signs: nausea, vomiting, irregular heartbeat (L2 s39);
anorexia and **yellow halos around objects** (L2 s72).

## 6. Procedures (L1 s61–66)

* **Balloon angioplasty**: balloon catheter widens a narrowed artery; usually with a
  **stent** (small metal mesh coil) to keep it open and reduce restenosis.
* **Pacemaker**: battery device under the skin, usually the left upper chest; leads to the
  heart (single chamber, dual chamber, biventricular). Treats slow rates and conduction
  problems → relieves fatigue, light-headedness, fainting.
* **CABG**: bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's saphenous vein, internal
  thoracic (mammary) artery or radial artery.

## 7. Coronary disease (L2 s6–19, s27–33)

* **Angina pectoris** (s6–11): a symptom of myocardial ischemia (demand > supply), usually
  from CAD. Tightness, pressure, fullness, squeezing in the centre of the chest; may radiate
  to the left back, arm, shoulder, neck or jaw; SOB and unexplained fatigue. **Stable**:
  predictable with exertion, stress, cold weather, large meals; lasts ≤ 15 minutes; relieved by
  rest or nitroglycerin. **Unstable**: occurs without cause (at rest), more serious, often
  **not relieved by nitroglycerin**. 12-lead ECG: inverted T (ischemia). Drugs: aspirin,
  nitrates, beta blockers, statins, CCBs, ACE inhibitors. Procedures: angioplasty, stent,
  CABG. Lifestyle: stop smoking, weight, activity, stress. PT: multiphase cardiac rehab for
  stable angina. **Levine's sign**: a clenched fist over the chest describing ischemic pain.
* **Atherosclerosis / arteriosclerosis** (s12–15): plaque narrows and stiffens arteries; caused
  by long-standing hypertension, diabetes, smoking. Symptoms depend on the artery: coronary →
  angina; cerebral → arm/leg numbness or weakness, slurred speech, facial droop; peripheral →
  intermittent claudication. Clots can cause ischemia, injury or death.
* **Aortic atherosclerosis** (s12): "silent"; mostly older men; after 50 most people have some.
  Fatty deposits → fibrous plaques; the aorta calcifies, becomes rigid with a small lumen →
  hypertension; emboli; aneurysm.
* **CAD** (s16–19): atherosclerotic plaque in the coronary arteries; slow, often silent for
  years. RCA supplies mainly the right atrium and ventricle; LCA the left. Risk factors:
  hypertension, diabetes, obesity, chronic kidney disease, high cholesterol and triglycerides,
  family history; **modifiable**: smoking, inactivity, alcohol abuse, stress. Partial
  occlusion → exertional SOB, weakness or angina; the first sign may be an MI. Tests: cardiac
  catheterization with coronary angiography (most accurate for location and severity); CT/MR
  angiograms (less invasive). Treatment: drugs as for angina; angioplasty, stent, CABG; PT:
  multiphase cardiac rehab.
* **Myocardial infarction** (s27–33): prolonged ischemia → injury → necrosis, from occlusion of
  one or more coronary arteries. Risk factors: personal/family history, smoking, inactivity,
  stress, hypertension, high cholesterol, diabetes, obesity; cocaine; aortic stenosis; more
  often in the morning. **Zones** (s28–29): infarction (necrotic, electrically inert) →
  **pathological Q waves**; injury → **ST elevation**; ischemia → **T-wave inversion**.
  **LAD thrombosis is the most common site** — the left ventricle ("widow maker").
  Transmural (full thickness) → STEMI / Q-wave MI; subendocardial (inner third) → NSTEMI.
  Presentation: ~2/3 have warning symptoms days–weeks before (unstable angina, SOB, fatigue);
  deep substernal pain or pressure, may radiate to jaw, left arm or back, **not relieved by
  rest or nitroglycerin**, may last hours; anxious, pale, sweating, fatigued, nausea and
  vomiting. Often atypical, **especially in women**; silent MI happens. Atypical signs (s31):
  unusual chest/stomach/abdominal pain, continuous mid-back (between the shoulder blades) or
  neck/shoulder pain, isolated right biceps pain, pain relieved by antacids, nausea and
  vomiting or flu-like symptoms without chest pain, unexplained intense anxiety, weakness or
  fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness. Diagnosis: 12-lead ECG; blood markers (§0). Treatment:
  stabilise; anticoagulants, beta blockers, thrombolytics, ACE inhibitors, vasodilators;
  angioplasty, stenting, endarterectomy, bypass; then cardiac rehab (low-level exercise,
  functional activity, relaxation, breathing, endurance, **continuous vital-sign monitoring**,
  risk-factor education).

## 8. Hypertension and aneurysm (L2 s2–5, s20–26)

* **Hypertension**: persistently raised BP. Primary — no identifiable cause, slow onset.
  Secondary — from another condition (pre-eclampsia, kidney disease, congenital vessel
  defects, atherosclerosis, stress) or medication. Systolic = pressure while the heart
  contracts; diastolic = while it rests. Risk rises with age; pregnancy, diabetes, sleep
  apnea; modifiable: obesity, inactivity, stress, tobacco, excess alcohol, excess sodium.
* **AHA blood-pressure categories** (s23): **Normal** < 120 *and* < 80 · **Elevated**
  120–129 *and* < 80 · **Stage 1** 130–139 *or* 80–89 · **Stage 2** ≥ 140 *or* ≥ 90 ·
  **Hypertensive crisis** > 180 *and/or* > 120 (consult a doctor immediately).
  **Isolated systolic hypertension**: high systolic with normal diastolic, common after 50.
* Usually **no symptoms** ("silent"); when very high: headache, confusion, visual changes or
  blurred vision, fatigue, arrhythmia, tinnitus, vertigo, flushed face, more urination at night.
* Management: risk-factor change, drugs (diuretics, beta blockers, CCBs, ACE inhibitors).
  PT: progressive exercise and education; maybe cardiac rehab. Poorly controlled HTN →
  metabolic syndrome, MI, heart failure, stroke (CVA), aneurysm, cognitive change, kidney
  disease, vision loss.
* **Aneurysm**: a localised balloon-like dilation of a weakened artery wall; aorta (thoracic
  — TAA, abdominal — AAA) and brain (Circle of Willis), any peripheral artery. Causes: smoking,
  hypertension, atherosclerosis, infection, genetics, trauma, age. **AAA** (most common; below
  the renal arteries): lower abdominal pain and dull low back pain; **rupture** → low back,
  abdominal or groin pain, nausea and vomiting, light-headedness, rapid heart rate →
  hypovolemic shock, usually fatal. **TAA**: jaw, neck, back or chest pain, cough, SOB.
  **Brain**: fatigue, loss of balance, speech and vision problems; rupture → subarachnoid
  hemorrhage: severe headache, loss of vision or double vision, loss of consciousness,
  vomiting, mental status change, seizure — an emergency. Imaging: ultrasound, CT, MRI, echo,
  angiography. Treatment: BP control; surgical graft repair of large aortic aneurysms.
  Popliteal aneurysms occur in 5–20 % of people with AAA.

## 9. Heart failure, pulmonary edema, cardiomyopathy, cor pulmonale (L2 s34–44, s68–72)

* **CHF**: the heart can't pump enough for the body's needs — a result of heart-muscle or
  valve disease, not a disease on its own. Causes: previous MI, valve incompetence, long-term
  hypertension, very low or high heart rates, AFib, PE, unstable angina, renal failure, high
  salt intake, severe anemia. Early sign: tachycardia. **Left-sided** (forward failure):
  blood backs up into the left atrium and lungs → pulmonary edema, cough, dyspnea (exertional
  first), **orthopnea**, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, tachycardia, crackles (rales),
  frothy pink sputum, S3, confusion, restlessness. **Right-sided** (backward failure):
  systemic venous congestion → **dependent pitting edema (ankles/pretibial first)**, **jugular
  venous distention**, ascites and abdominal distention, weight gain, right upper quadrant
  pain (liver congestion), anorexia and nausea, cyanosis of the nail beds. Either side can
  lead to the other. JVD reflects the right heart because no valve separates the SVC from the
  right atrium. Tests: urinalysis, CBC, electrolytes, TSH, BUN, creatinine, chest X-ray, ECG,
  echo (EF, filling pressures). Drugs: diuretics, nitrates, cardiac glycosides (digoxin),
  analgesics, ACE inhibitors — watch for **digitalis toxicity** (nausea, vomiting, irregular
  heartbeat). Exercise: individualised; walking to start; progress by heart rate and
  perceived exertion. Prognosis poor; end stage needs transplant.
* **Pulmonary edema** (s41–42): excess fluid in the lungs, usually from left-sided failure
  (also altitude, ARDS, PE, drugs, infection, smoke, toxins). **Acute = medical emergency**:
  extreme SOB, worse lying down, wheezing or gasping, anxiety, cough with frothy (possibly
  blood-tinged) sputum, chest pain, palpitations. Chronic: SOB worse lying down or with
  activity, wheeze, weight gain, leg swelling, fatigue. Confirmed by chest X-ray.
* **Cardiomyopathy** (s43–44): a group of heart-muscle diseases; may need transplant.
  Causes: heart attacks, alcoholism, long-term severe hypertension, SLE, end-stage renal
  disease, viral infection. **Dilated**: thinner muscle, enlarged chamber. **Hypertrophic**:
  thickened myocardium, especially the left ventricle. **Restrictive**: fibrous tissue stops
  the heart expanding. S/S: dyspnea, cough, palpitations, dependent edema, ascites, angina,
  dizziness, low urine output, mental changes, appetite loss, deconditioning. Complications:
  heart failure and arrhythmia.
* **Cor pulmonale** (s68–72): enlargement of the right ventricle from **pulmonary
  hypertension** caused by lung disease (emphysema, chronic bronchitis) or pulmonary vascular
  disease; most often adult male smokers. May show only with exercise (CO falls, ECG shows
  right ventricular hypertrophy). Symptoms mainly of the lung disease: chronic productive
  cough, exertional dyspnea, wheezing, fatigue, weakness. Diagnosis: exam, X-ray, ECG/echo;
  pulmonary function tests; **polycythemia** with COPD. Treatment: oxygen, salt and fluid
  restriction, diuretics, treat the lung disease; heart–lung transplant late. Prognosis poor
  (2–5 years once congestive signs appear). PT: reposition bed-bound patients (prevent
  atelectasis and skin breakdown); **diaphragmatic and pursed-lip breathing**; teach the
  patient/family to check ankle edema (press the shin 1–2 s, look for a lasting pit); watch
  for **digitalis toxicity** (anorexia, nausea, vomiting, yellow halos).

## 10. Peripheral vascular disease (L2 s45–56, s63–67)

* **PVD** (arteriosclerosis obliterans): narrowed vessels, mainly from atherosclerosis,
  plus emboli or thrombi. Contributing factors: atherosclerosis, phlebitis, injury/surgery,
  autoimmune disease, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, inactivity, hypertension, family
  history, age, obesity. Early: **intermittent claudication** (or the leg "giving out") relieved
  by rest. Later: tingling and numbness, **pain at rest and at night**, slow healing, **pale on
  elevation, dusky red when dependent (dependent rubor)**, cool skin, **hair loss**, weak or
  absent pulses. Tests: CBC, BUN, electrolytes, Doppler, MRI, angiogram, **ABI**, rubor of
  dependency, transcutaneous oximetry, treadmill test. Drugs: anticoagulants, antiplatelets,
  thrombolytics. Education: disease, limb protection, foot and skin care, stop smoking, avoid
  cold. **Walking programme: walk until near-maximal pain, rest until it eases, repeat; aim for
  30 minutes of continuous walking**; after 4–6 weeks add isometric, active and resistive
  exercise. Surgery: angioplasty, stent, bypass. Risks: permanent numbness, gangrene,
  amputation; higher risk of MI and stroke (≥ 30 % 5-year mortality when symptomatic).
* **Ankle-brachial index** (s48–50): systolic pressure at both brachial arteries and at the
  ankles (posterior tibial / dorsalis pedis) with a cuff and handheld Doppler. **ABI = the
  higher ankle pressure ÷ the higher arm pressure** (calculated for each leg).
  Interpretation: **> 1.30** non-compressible (calcified) vessel · **1.00–1.29** normal ·
  **0.91–0.99** borderline · **0.41–0.90** mild-to-moderate PAD (claudication) · **0.00–0.40**
  severe PAD (claudication early, rest pain).
* **Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger's disease)** (s52–53): inflammatory occlusion of small
  (and medium) arteries **and veins** of the hands and feet; young adults, mostly men, **who
  smoke or use tobacco**; starts distally and moves proximally; pain or paresthesias, cyanotic
  cold digits, reduced temperature sensation, fatigue, ulcers, gangrene (autoamputation).
  Stopping all tobacco is essential.
* **Diabetic angiopathy** (s54): high blood glucose + accelerated atherosclerosis;
  neuropathy is a major problem; neurotrophic ulcers → gangrene, amputation. Diabetic ulcers
  combine arterial and neuropathic problems: **painless** (sensory loss), slow to heal, typically
  on the **plantar surface of the foot**.
* **Raynaud's disease / phenomenon** (s55–56): women > men, ages 20–49; common with
  connective-tissue disease (scleroderma, RA, systemic sclerosis); acutely sensitive to cold;
  also emotional stress, smoking, vibrating machinery. Pale skin of the hands, cooler skin.
  Not usually serious but can cause ulcers of the fingers. Calcium channel blockers may be
  prescribed. **PT: no cold packs; aquatic therapy only if the pool is at least body
  temperature; avoid whirlpools at non-thermal temperatures.**
* **Chronic venous insufficiency** (s63–67): incompetent veins → venous hypertension →
  edema; protein-rich fluid → inflammation, fibrosis, **hemosiderin (brown) staining**, stasis
  ulcers. Heaviness and dull aching in the lower legs, **better with elevation**, worse when
  dependent. Venous ultrasonography.
* **Arterial vs venous insufficiency** (s65–66):

| | Chronic arterial insufficiency | Chronic venous insufficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | atherosclerosis, thrombosis, emboli, inflammation | thrombophlebitis, trauma, clot, incompetent valves |
| Pain | claudication: worse with exercise, relieved by rest; rest pain = severe; cramping, numbness | mild–moderate aching with prolonged standing or sitting (dependency) |
| Where | calf, lower leg, dorsum of foot; sometimes hip or buttock | superficial, along the vein |
| Vessels | decreased or absent pulses; pallor on elevation; dependent rubor | dilated veins; moderate–severe edema after dependency |
| Skin | pale, shiny, dry, hair loss, nail changes, cool | hemosiderin: dark, thickened brown skin; dermatitis, cellulitis |
| Ulcers | feet, toes, trauma sites; pale/yellow to black eschar; regular "punched-out"; gangrene possible | ankles, especially **medial malleolus**; shallow, exudative, irregular borders; painful; no gangrene |

## 11. Venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism (L2 s57–62)

* **Thrombophlebitis** = inflammation and clotting of a vein; **DVT** = clot in a deep vein,
  usually of the leg. Risk factors: prolonged immobility, recent surgery, pregnancy, obesity,
  smoking, birth-control pills and hormone replacement, polycythemia ("thick blood"),
  clotting disorders, cancer, autoimmune disease. Signs: **swelling, redness, warmth, pain**;
  tenderness, induration, fever; calf pain and swelling with popliteal DVT. **Homan's sign**
  (calf pain on dorsiflexion) is **not reliable**.
* **Wells score for DVT** (s59): active cancer (treatment within 6 months) +1; bedridden > 3
  days or major surgery within 12 weeks +1; calf swelling > 3 cm compared with the other leg
  (measured 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity) +1; collateral (non-varicose) superficial veins
  +1; entire leg swollen +1; localised tenderness along the deep veins +1; pitting edema
  confined to the symptomatic leg +1; paralysis, paresis or recent cast of the leg +1;
  previously documented DVT +1; an alternative diagnosis at least as likely −2.
  **< 2: DVT unlikely (≈ 6 %); ≥ 2: DVT likely (≈ 28 %).**
* Medical: Doppler ultrasound; anticoagulants; compression; complication — **PE, which can
  kill**. Prevention: early ambulation and exercise after surgery, elastic stockings,
  compression pumps. **PTA: note any lower-extremity pain, especially after surgery; take any
  unusual sign seriously** — sometimes the first sign is a PE.
* **Pulmonary embolism**: a clot (usually from leg veins) lodges in a pulmonary artery →
  poorly perfused, ischemic lung tissue. **Sudden dyspnea** (at rest and with activity),
  cough, hypoxia, **chest pain that can mimic MI** and **worsens with coughing, deep
  breathing**, eating or bending; blood-tinged sputum; unilateral leg edema; cyanosis,
  wheezing, sweating, fainting, rapid or weak pulse. Confirm with **V/Q scan or CT**.
  Thrombolytics/anticoagulants (bleeding risk).
